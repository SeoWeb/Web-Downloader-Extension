You found a page worth keeping. Maybe it is the documentation for a library
version your project still depends on, a news story you may need to cite, a
pricing page that is about to change, or a forum thread that solved a problem
nobody else has even hit yet. You want to be able to open it again in a year —
with the words, the images, and the layout intact — and find it again when the
moment comes.

The uncomfortable truth is that most ways people "save" web pages do not
survive that year. Links die, pages get redesigned or put behind paywalls,
sites shut down. Saving a page properly is a small skill, and choosing the
right method for the job makes the difference between a page you can rely on
and a bookmark that leads to a 404.

This guide walks through the methods people actually use, what each one
preserves and what it silently throws away, and the checklist we think any
long-term save should pass.

## What "saving a web page" should mean

Before comparing methods, it helps to be precise about the goal. A page you
have truly saved is a copy that is:

- **Faithful** — the text, images, and layout come back the way the page
  looked when you saved it, not a mangled or partial reconstruction.
- **Under your control** — it opens even if the original site edits the page,
  moves it, or disappears entirely, and nobody else can edit or revoke it.
- **Findable** — you can locate it later by searching the words inside it,
  not by remembering which folder or bookmark folder you put it in.
- **Shareable** — when someone else needs to see it, you can hand them the
  saved copy without sending them to a page that may have changed.

Every method below can be judged against those four properties.

## The methods people actually use

### Bookmarks: saving the address, not the page

A bookmark stores a URL. It saves none of the page itself — no text, no
images, no layout. When the page changes, your bookmark quietly points at
something different from what you saved; when the page dies, it points at
nothing. Bookmarks are excellent for remembering where to go back to, and
worthless as an archive. If the page matters, a bookmark is where the job
starts, not where it ends.

### Print to PDF: readable, but rarely faithful

Every browser can print a page to a PDF, and the result is genuinely useful:
the text survives, the file is portable, and PDFs are easy to store. But a
print-PDF is a re-rendering, not a capture. Layouts get reflowed or broken,
sticky headers and cookie banners can end up stamped across pages, content
that loads on scroll may never make it into the file, and anything interactive
— embedded videos, maps, tabs — is reduced to whatever happened to be visible.
For reading an article offline, a PDF is fine. As a record of what a page
actually looked like, it is unreliable in ways that only show up when it is
too late.

### Screenshots: proof of a moment, silent on the words

A screenshot is honest visual evidence and costs nothing to take. The
weakness is the flip side: a screenshot is an image, so the words inside it
are not text. You cannot search them, copy a quotation out of them, or
structurally compare two captures. Long articles become a pile of images you
have to scroll through, and six months later you cannot remember which pile
holds the paragraph you need. Screenshots work well as a quick receipt of what
a page showed — paired with a full copy of the page, they are even better.

### "Save Page As": a snapshot stranded on one machine

Browsers offer *Save Page As…*, producing either a folder of files or a
single MHTML file. This is the most faithful of the local methods: the HTML,
the styles, and usually the images come down exactly as served. The problems
start after the save. Pages that build themselves with scripts often reassemble
incorrectly when opened from disk. Your saves land in a downloads folder,
indistinguishable from the PDF manual you grabbed last week. There is no way
to search across everything you have saved, no way to share a save except by
sending files around, and nothing follows you to another device. It is a good
raw snapshot with no archive around it.

### Public web archives: powerful, but not yours

Services like the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine capture pages
automatically and keep those captures publicly available, which is genuinely
valuable — for popular pages, a capture of a given moment may already exist.
But an automatic public archive is, by design, not under your control. Whether
a capture exists for the page and date you need is luck. Dynamic,
personalised, paywalled, or rarely visited pages are frequently captured badly
or not at all. You cannot organise captures into collections, attach notes,
or hand someone a private copy. Public archives are a wonderful safety net for
the web at large — treating them as your personal archive means outsourcing
your record-keeping to a crawler.

## A checklist for a page you truly keep

Whichever tool you use, a save worth trusting should pass this list:

1. **Opens offline** — the copy renders from storage, with no live calls back
   to the original site.
2. **Full fidelity** — text is real text, images are present, layout matches
   the original.
3. **Searchable in bulk** — the words inside every page you have saved can be
   searched from one place.
4. **Organised** — pages land in collections you define, not in a downloads
   folder.
5. **Sharable on your terms** — you can produce a link to the saved copy when
   you choose to, and keep it private otherwise.
6. **Durable** — the copy lives somewhere that survives a new laptop, a
   reinstalled browser, and a changed original page.

Local tools cover points 1 and 2 at best. Public archives cover durability
but not control. Very few workflows cover all six.

## Choosing the right method for the job

No single method wins every scenario, and you do not need one to:

- **Just need to find the page again later?** A bookmark is fine — accept that
  you are trusting the site to keep the page as it is.
- **Reading an article on a flight?** Print to PDF.
- **Need proof of what a page showed at a moment?** A screenshot, or better,
  a full capture plus a screenshot.
- **The page is evidence — cited in work, referenced in a dispute, part of a
  project?** You need a controlled, faithful, searchable copy that belongs to
  you. That is the case for a personal archive like PagePocket.
