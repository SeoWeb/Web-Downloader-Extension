Citing a web page means asking a stranger to keep a promise: that the
page will still be there, unchanged, whenever a reader follows your
footnote. The web makes no such promise. Pages get edited, paywalled,
moved, and deleted — and citations rot accordingly.

This article is about making citations that hold. It explains what
Perma.cc is and why it exists, is clear about when it is the right tool
(often), and then covers what the rest of us — researchers, journalists,
analysts, students — can use when formal citation infrastructure is more
than the job requires.

## Citations rot faster than paper ever did

The scale is documented, not anecdotal. A [Pew Research Center
analysis](https://www.pewresearch.org/data-labs/2024/05/17/when-online-content-disappears/)
found that 38% of webpages that existed in 2013 were no longer accessible
a decade later.

For legal writing, the problem has its own landmark study. In
["Perma: Scoping and Addressing the Problem of Link and Reference Rot in
Legal Citations"](https://harvardlawreview.org/forum/vol-127/perma-scoping-and-addressing-the-problem-of-link-and-reference-rot-in-legal-citations/)
(Harvard Law Review Forum, 2014), Jonathan Zittrain, Kendra Albert, and
Lawrence Lessig found that more than 70% of URLs in the Harvard Law
Review and similar journals — and around 50% of URLs in United States
Supreme Court opinions — no longer linked to the originally cited
information. A citation to a dead or changed page is a claim a reader
cannot check, which for law is close to no citation at all.

## What Perma.cc is — and when it is the right tool

Perma.cc was created directly in response to that problem. It is a
citation-archiving service built and supported by libraries, with
records preserved by the Harvard Law School Library. You give it a URL;
it captures the page and returns a permanent record — a Perma Link —
that courts, journals, and readers cite instead of the fragile original.
Law reviews ask authors to attach them to cited URLs, and courts cite
them in opinions; the raw link in your footnote becomes a pointer to the
frozen copy rather than a promise about the live page.

The [Perma.cc documentation](https://perma.cc/docs/accounts) and
[sign-up page](https://perma.cc/sign-up) describe the access model:
members of registrar institutions — many courts, law schools, and
universities — get unlimited free service through their library, and new
individual accounts receive a small batch of trial links. Beyond that,
individual plans start at $10 a month for 10 new links, with larger
monthly tiers ($25 for 100 links, $100 for 500) and one-time link packs
available.

Be clear about what that buys you, because it is unique: **third-party,
institutional permanence**. The record lives in a registry maintained by
libraries — not in your account, your laptop, or any company's goodwill
toward a customer. When a citation carries legal weight, that is the
guarantee you want, and Perma.cc is the recognised standard for it.

So the honest guidance: if you are filing in court, editing a law
review, or citing formally in academic work, and you have access through
an institution, use Perma.cc. PagePocket will not pretend to compete
with that — our [comparison page](/compare/perma-cc) says the same thing
in a table.

## What everyone else actually needs

Most citation-rot victims are not filing briefs. A researcher's
literature review, a journalist's source list, an analyst's report, a
student's essay — all cite pages that can change, but none of them needs
a library-grade registry for every link. What they need is:

- a **faithful copy** of the page as it was when cited;
- **privacy by default**, with material visible only to them until they
  choose otherwise;
- the copy **findable later** — by searching what was written on it,
  not by re-tracing a bookmark;
- and **selective sharing**: a stable link for collaborators or readers
  when the moment comes.

For the daily job — the paper due next week, the report with forty
references, the archive of sources behind a series — the questions are
different: does the copy exist, is it faithful, can you find it again,
can you hand it to someone? Perma.cc answers those for individual links
too, at per-link prices; a personal archive answers them for your whole
library at once.

## A rule of thumb

- **Formal legal or scholarly citation?** Perma.cc — through your
  institution's registrar access if you have it. It exists precisely so
  those citations do not rot.
- **The page should become public record?** The [Wayback Machine's Save
  Page Now](https://web.archive.org/save/) gives a free, citable public
  URL — complements both tools above.
- **Everything else — your sources, research, receipts, and drafts?** A
  private archive you control: faithful copies, full-text search, and
  share links when you decide something is ready to show.

One habit covers the gaps between them: when a page starts to matter,
capture it *before* you need it. Every tool in this article only works
on the pages you thought to keep. The [journalist's
workflow](/blog/journalists-archive-sources-link-rot) shows what that
looks like in practice, and [Wayback Machine alternatives for personal
archiving](/blog/wayback-machine-alternatives-personal-archiving)
compares the wider field.
