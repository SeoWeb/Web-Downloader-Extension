People usually arrive at the phrase "Wayback Machine alternative" after
the same small disaster: a page they needed has changed or vanished, and
the copy that was supposed to exist somewhere either does not — or it
exists, but it is not theirs to use. The instinct to look for a
replacement is understandable. It is also slightly misdirected.

The Wayback Machine is not a bad tool that needs replacing. It is one of
the most important projects on the web. The useful question is narrower:
which parts of "keeping pages" it was ever designed to do for you, and
which tools cover the rest. This article walks through the realistic
options — archive.today, SingleFile, Perma.cc, your browser's own save,
and PagePocket — and states plainly what each one is good at, including
the cases where we would tell you to use something else.

## What the Wayback Machine is genuinely great at

Any honest comparison has to start here. The Wayback Machine is run by
the Internet Archive, a non-profit, and it preserves the web for
everyone, free of charge. Its
[Save Page Now](https://web.archive.org/save/) captures the page you give
it and returns a permanent web.archive.org URL that anyone can open,
cite, and share — as the [Internet Archive's own
documentation](https://help.archive.org/help/save-pages-in-the-wayback-machine/)
describes. Beyond manual saves, it holds years of crawl history for
popular URLs: a record of how pages changed over time that no personal
tool can reconstruct.

If your goal is "this page should exist somewhere public, for everyone,
indefinitely", the Wayback Machine remains the right answer. Nothing in
this article changes that.

## Where a public archive stops being yours

The limitations are not defects — they are what "public archive" means:

- **Captures are not guaranteed.** What the Wayback Machine holds comes
  from crawls and manual saves. Whether a capture exists for the exact
  page and date you need is a matter of luck, and dynamic, personalised,
  paywalled, or rarely visited pages are frequently captured badly or not
  at all.
- **It is public by design.** Anyone can view the collections, and site
  owners can request that pages be
  [excluded or removed](https://help.archive.org/help/using-the-wayback-machine/).
  A page you depend on can leave the archive without your involvement.
- **There is no private library.** Signing in to Save Page Now lets you
  see your captures listed under "My Web Archive", but the saves
  themselves join the shared public archive — there are no private
  collections, no notes, no organisation around *your* work.
- **There is no search across your own captures.** The Wayback Machine
  helps you locate archived URLs. It does not index the contents of the
  pages you personally saved so you can find that paragraph you
  half-remember.

For the web at large, those trade-offs are worth it. For a personal
archive — the pages your work, research, or record-keeping depends on —
they are exactly the wrong trade-offs. That gap is what the alternatives
below try to fill.

## The alternatives, honestly

### archive.today: anonymous public snapshots

[archive.today](https://archive.ph/) is a free archive that needs no
account: you submit a URL and it produces a snapshot on its own domain.
Captures are public, and the site is ad-supported. It has operated under
several domain names over the years — archive.ph, archive.is, archive.md,
among others — so links to captures may point at a different mirror than
the one you originally bookmarked.

It is a reasonable pick when you want a quick public copy of a single
page. Like the Wayback Machine, it has no accounts, which means no
private library, no collections, and no way to search across the pages
*you* have saved. It archives pages; it does not keep an archive for you.

### SingleFile: excellent files, but a folder is not an archive

[SingleFile](https://www.getsinglefile.com/) is a free, open-source
browser extension that saves an entire page — images, stylesheets, fonts
— as one standard HTML file that opens offline in any browser, today or
in twenty years. We think it is the best local tool there is, and our
[SingleFile comparison](/compare/singlefile) says so plainly rather than
pretending otherwise.

What it does not give you is the layer above files. Saves land in a
folder on your machine; keeping them synced, backed up, organised, and
findable is entirely your job, and there is no way to share a save except
by sending the file itself. We cover what that costs at real volume in
[SingleFile vs a cloud archive](/blog/singlefile-vs-cloud-archive).

### Perma.cc: permanence for citations

[Perma.cc](https://perma.cc/) is built and supported by libraries, with
records preserved by the Harvard Law School Library, and it exists for
one job: turning cited web sources into permanent records that do not
rot — primarily for courts, law reviews, and academic writing. Through
its [accounts documentation](https://perma.cc/docs/accounts), many
courts and members of registrar institutions get unlimited free service;
individual plans otherwise start at $10 a month for 10 links.

If your pages are citations in formal legal or scholarly work, Perma.cc
is the recognised standard — we explain when and why in [how to cite web
pages that won't rot](/blog/cite-web-pages-that-wont-rot). For everyday
archiving at working volume, its per-link pricing model is built for a
different job than yours.

### Your browser's "Save Page As": quick, forgettable

The save built into every browser produces a folder of files or a single
MHTML file. For a one-off copy you will read this week, it is fine. As an
archive it fails quietly: saves land in a downloads folder
indistinguishable from every other download, nothing is searchable across
saves, nothing follows you to another device, and script-heavy pages
often reassemble incorrectly when opened from disk.

### PagePocket: a private Wayback Machine for your pages

[PagePocket](/features) is our answer to the gap above. A browser
extension saves the full page — complete HTML, styles, images — with one
click, and the copy lands in your private cloud library instead of a
public archive. From there:

- pages are **private by default**; nothing is public unless you create a
  share link, and links can expire or be revoked;
- every word of every save is **full-text searchable**, so a page you
  half-remember is one query away;
- **collections and notes** keep projects and stories organised;
- share links give anyone the **exact copy you saved**, which cannot be
  edited under their feet.

There is a [free plan](/pricing) — 50 pages a month, 500 MB — and Pro is
$2/month billed yearly when your archive outgrows it. To be plain about
the trade-offs: it requires an account, it is a cloud service that needs
a connection, and saving privately contributes nothing to the public
record. Those are the costs of the opposite design from the Wayback
Machine, not a claim that the trade-offs do not exist.

## Which alternative should you pick?

- **The save should be public record?** The [Wayback
  Machine](/compare/wayback) — free, citable, for everyone.
- **A quick anonymous public copy of one page?** archive.today.
- **A copy that must open offline, from disk, forever?**
  [SingleFile](/compare/singlefile) — genuinely unbeatable at that.
- **A citation in legal or academic work that must never break?**
  [Perma.cc](/compare/perma-cc), ideally through your institution.
- **The pages you personally depend on — private, organised, searchable,
  shareable on your terms?** That is [PagePocket](/features).

These tools are not rivals so much as layers. Reporters keep a private
archive of sources *and* publish public receipts; researchers keep local
copies of datasets *and* a searchable library of papers. Picking one tool
for all four jobs is where archives go to die.
