A web source is on the record until it suddenly is not. The company edits
the blog post after publication. The press release moves behind a new CMS
and loses its old URL. The local news site that broke the story shuts
down. The page is fine at 9 a.m. and rewritten by lunch — and your
quote is in a published story that points at the live URL.

This article is about the professional version of the problem: what a
newsroom-grade source archive needs to contain, and a concrete workflow
for it — save the source, organise by story, search across everything,
and hand your editor a link to the exact copy you saw.

## What actually happens to sources

The phenomenon is easy to describe without exaggeration, because it has
been measured. A [Pew Research Center analysis from May
2024](https://www.pewresearch.org/data-labs/2024/05/17/when-online-content-disappears/)
found that 38% of webpages that existed in 2013 were no longer accessible
a decade later, and that 23% of news webpages contain at least one broken
link. A quarter of all pages that existed at some point between 2013 and
2023 are gone.

Not every loss is a deletion. Sources also rot while technically
surviving:

- **Quiet edits** — a page is updated after publication, and the sentence
  you relied on is rewritten or gone.
- **Paywalls and region blocks** — the page loads for you today and for
  no one you send it to tomorrow.
- **Redesigns and migrations** — the URL changes, and redirects die at
  the next replatforming.
- **Takedowns and shutdowns** — legal complaints, corporate PR cycles,
  and small sites going dark all remove pages entirely.

When any of these happens to a source in your story, the failure is not
abstract: the quote becomes unverifiable, the editor asking "where did
this come from?" gets a shrug, the fact-checker stalls, and if a subject
disputes your characterisation of a page, you have nothing to show. Your
citations are only as strong as the worst thing that has happened to
their URLs since you reported the story.

## What a source receipt has to do

Before the workflow, the requirements — because a screenshot on your
phone does not meet them:

1. **Faithful.** The words, images, and layout come back the way the page
   looked when you saved it — not a re-rendered approximation.
2. **Timestamped.** You can say what the page showed on the day you
   captured it.
3. **Private until you decide.** Sources for an unpublished story must
   not be sitting in a public archive anyone can browse.
4. **Shareable in one click.** Your editor opens the exact copy from a
   link, without an account on your tools and without you sending files.
5. **Findable months later.** When the follow-up lands, you need every
   source from every related story — searchable, not archaeology.
6. **Independent of the live site.** The copy opens even when the
   original is edited, moved, paywalled, or deleted.

## A workflow that survives editors and deadlines

Here is the same flow we walk through on the [journalists use-case
page](/use-cases/journalists), run with [PagePocket](/features).

### 1. Save the source at first contact

The moment a page matters — the executive's old statement, the forum post
with the detail everyone missed, the competitor's pricing page — save it
with the browser extension. One click captures a full-fidelity copy into
your private library. The rule is simple: if you might cite it, capture
it before you close the tab. Saving after publication is archaeology;
saving at first contact is reporting.

### 2. One collection per story

As you report, saves land in a collection named for the story —
"council rezoning vote", " merger filing", whatever your shorthand is.
When the story is fact-checked, everything the piece rests on is in one
place: articles, press releases, regulatory filings, the deleted tweet's
page. No digging through browser history to reconstruct where a paragraph
came from.

### 3. Note the why

Attach a note to the saves that need explaining: which sentence matters,
what to verify, who claimed what. Future-you, or the colleague picking up
the follow-up, needs the reason the page was worth capturing — the page
itself cannot tell them.

### 4. Search across everything when the story moves

Six months later the follow-up lands, and you half-remember a detail from
a source you can no longer find. Because every word of every save is
full-text searchable, a phrase from the original surfaces the page —
across every story you have covered, not just the one collection.

### 5. Hand your editor the exact copy

When an editor or fact-checker needs to see a source, create a share
link to the saved copy. They see precisely what you saw — the page
cannot be edited under their feet between your read and theirs — and the
link can carry an expiry date. Meanwhile nothing becomes public: the
library stays private, and only the pages you deliberately share have
links at all.

### 6. On publication, keep the public record too

For sources the public should be able to check, a public archive earns
its place: the [Wayback Machine's Save Page
Now](https://web.archive.org/save/) turns a page into a citable public
URL in seconds. Private working archive for the reporting, public
receipts for the readership — the two are complements, and using both is
the professional default. We compare the two approaches honestly in
[Wayback Machine alternatives for personal
archiving](/blog/wayback-machine-alternatives-personal-archiving).

The honest limits: PagePocket requires an account, works online, and has

editor, the lawyer, or the subject of the story comes asking — a private,
searchable archive you control is the tool that fits.
